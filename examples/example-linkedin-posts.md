## Post 1

**Hook test:** Yes — anyone who's inherited a system they never signed up for stops here.

---

Nobody applied for the job of "the person who understands the system."

You just... became it. One day something broke, you happened to fix it, and now
every question routes to you. It's not in your title. It's not what you were
hired for. But it's yours now.

If you go on vacation, does it survive? If you leave, does anyone else know how it
works?

the only one who understands your own systems.

You shouldn't have to become an accidental expert just to keep the lights on.

## Post 2

**Angle:** The silent failure
**Pain anchor:** Poor support — tickets sit for days while work stops
**Hook test:** Yes — the specificity of "lost a customer to a silent failure" lands.

---

The most expensive failures are the quiet ones.

Not the ones that page you at 2am — those get fixed. The dangerous ones are the
failures nobody notices until a customer does.

Something breaks silently. No alert. No error anyone catches. Work just... stops
flowing in the background. And by the time you find out, the damage is already
done and sitting in your inbox with an angry subject line.

Then you open a support ticket. And you wait. Days, sometimes.

Here's the thing: when your work stops, "we'll get back to you" isn't support.
It's abandonment with a ticket number.

The question to ask any vendor isn't "do you have support?" Everyone says yes.

It's: "when something breaks and my business stops, does a human pick up?"

## Post 3

**Angle:** Contrarian take on switching costs
**Pain anchor:** Migration fear + unpredictable pricing
**Hook test:** Medium-high — the reframe ("the risk isn't switching") is the hook.

---

"We can't switch right now — it's too risky."

I hear this constantly. And I get it. Switching feels dangerous, especially near
a busy season or a hard deadline. Nobody wants to be the person who broke the
thing that was mostly working.

But let's name the other risk, the one that doesn't feel like a decision because
it's just... the status quo:

→ A bill that changes every month with fees you didn't plan for.
→ One person who holds all the knowledge.
→ Support that treats you like a small account because you are one, to them.

Staying isn't safe. It's just a risk you've gotten used to.

The real question isn't "should we risk switching?" It's "which risk do we
actually want — the one-time risk of a careful migration, or the forever-risk of
staying stuck?"

A good switch is boring. It's planned, it's supported, and nothing blows up.
That's not luck. That's the whole job.
